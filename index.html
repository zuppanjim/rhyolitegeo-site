The site

Picked by what was already there

Glass Ball sits beside I-40, 2.3 miles east of Henryetta. ONEOK's gas line runs 2.47 miles to the north-west, and PSO's Henryetta 138 kV substation is 2.29 miles away for construction power. A grow facility was planned on the parcel in 2018 and never built.

The county site screen ranked two nearby spots at 82 and 80 out of 100 on resources. They stand behind Glass Ball as alternates.

Why fuel cells

At this size, the wire and the engines both run out

At a 77.2 MW peak the hall is above Oklahoma's 75 MW large load line, where the queue is about five years and a large transformer alone takes three to four. Engines at this load cross the formaldehyde line where the air permit changes class.

Fuel cells make no formaldehyde and come up one stamp at a time.

How it is powered

Gas pipe to rack, with nothing in between

Every row of 16 racks has its own stamp of ten 325 kW skids feeding an 800 volt DC row bus. A row runs on eight of its ten skids, so the two spares are the backup. Ultracapacitors on each bus carry the GPUs' swings while the stacks ramp.

Each pod has a second stamp for its cooling. Twelve yards of dry condensers reject the heat by thermosyphon, with no process water.

Why pair them

The plant and a battery on one bus

A data center is the least forgiving load there is: continuous, indifferent to the weather, and expected to hold better than 99.99% uptime. Beside a geothermal plant there is no pipeline to lean on, so the ground carries the base and a battery system carries everything that has to happen fast.

Both sit on the same 800 volt DC ring. The binary unit runs flat out as the base. The battery takes the swings a training run puts on the bus, rides through a well or a turbine dropping out while the plant rebalances, and carries the hall through maintenance. It is sized for speed and ride-through, not to store a night, because the ground does not set the way the sun does.

Concept. Geothermal fluid stays in a closed loop and is reinjected; nothing is vented.

Dual use

The same resource that powers the racks also cools them

Cooling is 30 to 40% of what a data center consumes, and a power contract normally cannot touch it. Unless the power comes from the ground.

ABSORPTION

Waste heat drives the chillers

Heat from generation runs absorption chillers directly, displacing electric mechanical cooling, and pays back hardest in the hot climates where conventional cooling is worst.

PUE

From 1.4–1.8 toward 1.03

Full geothermal integration in a cold climate approaches 1.03. Captured properly, powering and cooling from one resource improves project economics by 20 to 40%.

Fuel cost

Zero, and it stays zero for thirty to fifty years

The heat source is the Earth. There is no commodity to hedge and no fuel escalator to negotiate. Operations and maintenance run 2 to 5% of installed cost a year and do not move with a fuel market or a rate case.

With no gas line to the site, no fuel price appears anywhere in the power cost. Every megawatt-hour comes from the ground, and the battery only moves it in time.

An ordinary resource temperature across a great deal of the western United States. The resource does not have to be exotic to be useful.

CLOSED LOOP

A secondary working fluid turns the turbine and the geothermal fluid is fully reinjected, so direct emissions are near zero.

THE RIGHT SIZE

1 to 50 MW a unit

Not a plant a data center has to be scaled up to meet. It is a plant a data center already fits.

High-pressure geothermal

One well, three streams: heat, pressure and gas

Deep under the Gulf Coast, hot brine sits sealed at pressures well above hydrostatic, carrying natural gas dissolved in it. A geopressured well brings all three to the surface at once, and a hybrid plant turns every one of them into power. No pipeline comes in: the only gas is what the brine carries up, burned at the wellhead.

HEAT

Hot brine to a binary unit

The brine leaves the separator hot enough to run a closed-loop binary turbine, then goes back down the injection well.

GAS

Methane out of solution

Gas that breaks out of the brine at the separator fuels engines on site. Their exhaust heat goes back into the binary cycle instead of up the stack.

PRESSURE

Energy at the wellhead

The brine arrives under high pressure, a third stream a hydraulic turbine can take ahead of the separator.

Schematic · the hybrid layout DOE ran at Pleasant Bayou, Texas

It has been built and run

The U.S. Department of Energy ran a hybrid geopressured plant at Pleasant Bayou on the Texas Gulf Coast from October 1989 to May 1990, gas engines and a binary turbine on one brine stream, selling power to the local utility. The run ended because the injection well needed rework, not the plant.

How we develop it

The heat is developed in phases, against the racks it has to carry

The hall is fast because it repeats. The resource is slow because it has to be proven, and pretending otherwise is how geothermal projects get into trouble. So the first halls go beside plants already generating, and the battery, not a gas line, smooths each step up as a phase comes on.

  1. FIRST

    Screen on paper

    Temperature at depth, the wells and surveys somebody already paid for, the output a plant has to spare, and the distance to the fiber. A resource is ranked before any land is taken.

  2. THEN

    Start beside what is proven

    Confirming a reservoir is the expensive step, so the first pairings sit beside plants already generating, where somebody else drilled and proved it years ago.

  3. AS THE HALL FILLS

    Drill to the load

    Wellfield phases follow building phases, each triggered by how full the last one is, so drilled capacity and installed racks move together. Racks come on only against output already proven.

Why the farm's bus

The connection is already built

A renewable farm is built around its interconnection: a substation, a collector bus and a queue position that took years to win. On a bright, windy afternoon it can make more than the line will take. A hall on the same bus uses those megawatts at the fence.

The racks do not care about the weather. They draw the same load at noon and at three in the morning, so the farm is never asked to be firm. Fuel cells on a gas lateral sit beside the hall and follow the gap: they carry whatever the sun and the wind do not, and all of it on a still night.

Every megawatt-hour the farm delivers is a megawatt-hour of gas the fuel cells do not burn.

Concept. Sun and wind run the cooling and the 480 VAC loads through the farm bus; the fuel cells feed the 800 VDC ring, and the ring feeds only the 800 VDC equipment.

A day on the bus

The weather changes all day. The load does not.

MIDDAY

The sun carries the hall

Solar output peaks while the racks draw flat. The fuel cells turn down to their floor, and what the hall does not use goes out through the farm's interconnection as it always has.

EVENING

The wind takes the handoff

On many sites the wind rises as the sun sets. Whatever the two give, the fuel cells make up the difference, so the racks never see the change.

STILL NIGHT

The gas carries everything

No sun and no wind. The fuel cells carry the full load on their own, which is why they are sized for the whole hall and not for the gap.

Why spread it out

A gigawatt in one place puts every risk in one place too

A single giant campus needs one interconnect sized to a city, one water draw, one town to absorb years of construction, and it sits under one storm track. When it waits, all of it waits. When it goes down, all of it goes down.

Spread across sites, each of those is a fraction. A site that fits the gas lateral or the geothermal unit already beside it gets built at that size, and comes online when its own power does, not when a gigawatt transmission upgrade finally clears.

Every data-parallel training step ends in a ring all-reduce: each shard passes to the next rank and around. We number the ranks in ring order, so every logical hop is a physical nearest-neighbour hop and no span carries two flows. Cut a span and the ring closes the other way, at a longer term.
