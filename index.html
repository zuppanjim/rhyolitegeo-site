Company information

What the company is, and what it builds

Rhyolite Geo Inc. develops two things that belong together: geothermal power, and the small data centers that run on it. Heat is the one resource that cannot be shipped, so the building goes to it instead. Sites are alternative energy generation that is already in operation, most often geothermal, where the output is firm around the clock and the same resource that makes the power also carries the cooling. Each facility is a little different, tailored to site-specific conditions: roughly 3,000 square feet, standardized inside down to the last detail and repeated site to site, carrying a load that starts at 500 kW, in a building we put up or one that already stands. A site is phased up to 2 MW by repeating that building, rather than by making one of them larger.

The company is a facilitator, and wears whichever hats a project needs: site selection and diligence, development, plans and specifications, construction management, commissioning, and the long-run maintenance and operation of the finished building. On its own projects it builds, owns and operates rather than selling them on, and sells capacity locally by the cabinet and the partial cage. Where a host or a landowner wants only part of that, we take only that part. The team has run every one of these stages before, on data centers considerably larger than these.

Where the company stands

Where the company actually stands

Rhyolite Geo Inc. is a startup. We do not operate facilities today. We are searching sites, developing the first of them, and settling the standard kit that gets repeated after that.

We would rather say that plainly than sound further along than we are.

Small, meshed data centers. Capacity measured in hundreds of kilowatts, placed near the people and the work it serves, and made large by repetition across many sites rather than by one campus. The demand for it is arriving now, from regional enterprises and from inference that has to answer close to the person asking.

Geothermal developed for this load specifically. Not generation built for a grid and sold into it, but a resource developed against what a data center actually needs — firm output around the clock, and the cooling that comes with the same resource.

Existing alternative energy sites, taken locally. Plants that are already permitted, interconnected and running, where power can be taken on site instead of moved. The slow work was finished by somebody else years ago, and the opportunity is site by site rather than national.

Each of those three is a market in its own right and none of them is crowded yet. What makes the combination worth building a company around is that the same modest building serves all three, and that the timing on all three is the same timing. The load is growing faster than firm power is being added, the grid queue has stopped being a realistic path for anyone in a hurry, and the operating clean plants that could host this are sitting there today with nobody asking.

That window is open now. It does not stay open indefinitely, and being early to it is the whole thesis of the company.

Small enough to be welcome

Nobody turns out to fight a building this small

Through 2026 the argument over data centers stopped being abstract and started showing up on council agendas. Indianapolis paused new data center permits through the end of 2027. Montgomery County, Maryland passed its moratorium on an eleven to nothing council vote. Hillsboro, Oregon stopped new and expanded projects for a hundred and twenty days, unanimously. Tucson wrote its first zoning rules for large sites, and the list of what those rules demand is a fair summary of what people are actually worried about: setbacks from houses, limits on noise, and disclosure of how much water and how much power the building is going to take.

None of that was aimed at a building like ours. It was aimed at campuses measured in hundreds of acres and hundreds of megawatts, drawing cooling water from an aquifer the county was already arguing about and asking a utility for more power than it has spare. Three thousand square feet next to a plant that is already generating is a different conversation entirely. The setback question, the noise question and the water question are all easy for us to answer, and we would rather answer them early than win them later.

That is also why we get large the other way around. Rather than one campus that rearranges the town it lands in, we put the same modest building on a lot of different sites, and the size of the company shows up in the total instead of in any one place. It is a slower way to be big and a much easier one to be welcome.

We would honestly rather the buildings be boring. If one of our sites turns out to be the most interesting thing on its road, something has gone wrong.

The resource

The heat is developed in phases, against the racks it has to carry

The building half of this is fast because it repeats. The resource half is slow because it has to be proven, and pretending otherwise is how geothermal projects get into trouble.

The screen happens on paper first. Temperature at depth, the wells and surveys somebody has already paid for, and the distance to an interconnection and to fiber. A resource is ranked before any land is taken, because the cheapest well is the one that is never drilled in the wrong place.

Confirmation is the expensive step, so the first projects do not take it. The slow money in geothermal is proving the reservoir. Siting beside a plant that is already generating skips that step: the resource was confirmed, drilled and paid for by somebody else years ago, and the output is already firm.

Where the resource is developed, it is drilled to the load. Wellfield phases are matched to building phases rather than run ahead of them, and each phase is triggered by how full the last one is — the same rule the site build-out follows — so drilled capacity and installed racks move together instead of one waiting years on the other.

This is the half of the business that cannot be made fast, and it is why the first sites are the ones where the slow work is already finished. What the resource buys in return is what a data center actually needs and the grid is short of: output that does not move with the weather or the time of day, a fuel cost that stays at zero for decades, and cooling from the same ground that makes the power.

The one we do not control

Network diversity without waiting on a second carrier

Everything else on that list is ours to build. Fiber is not: a second carrier arrives when a carrier decides it does, and at the kind of site we want, that decision often never comes. So the second path is engineered rather than purchased, and it does not have to be fiber.

The lit carrier is bought on a protected ring, not as a point-to-point circuit. A cut on one side of the ring heals on the other, on the carrier’s own alternate route, without us owning a second one. This is ordinary carrier product and it is the first place diversity should come from, because it is the cheapest.

The second path is a different medium. Licensed point-to-point radio to the nearest carrier hotel or point of presence needs line of sight and a license, not a trench, a franchise agreement or a construction season. It is the piece that answers a site where fiber is genuinely hard, and it can be standing before the building is finished.

The host plant is already connected, and nobody counts it. An operating generating facility runs telemetry, protection and dispatch communications, and there is frequently utility-owned fiber on the transmission corridor it sits on. That is a second physical route out of the property that exists because of where we site, and it is asked for during diligence rather than discovered later.

Control does not ride the same paths as the traffic. Out-of-band management over satellite means the facility stays reachable when both data paths are down, so a network failure is a service interruption and never a site nobody can see into.

The trench and the second entrance still go in on day one, because conduit is cheap in an open trench and expensive through a finished floor. What changed is that nothing waits on them. And at the far end of the argument, a tenant with racks in more than one of these buildings has the redundancy that matters most: the sites are small, meshed and independent, so a facility that loses its network is a fraction of a footprint, not the footprint.

What it removes

The three things that make everyone else wait

No queue position. We never apply for interconnection, so there is no queue to enter and no position to wait out. The power never reaches transmission.

No utility equipment order. Transformers and switchgear carry the worst lead times in the industry. A site on this side of the meter never orders them, because the host plant's equipment is already installed and energized.

No siting fight of our own. The host settled what belongs on that land years ago, when the plant was permitted. We build inside a decision someone else has already won.

Geothermal development

The only renewable that is still running at four in the morning

Global data center electricity demand is projected to double by 2030. Almost everything capable of meeting it either burns fuel or stops when the weather does. One resource does neither.

Geothermal produces from heat that is already in the ground. There is no fuel to buy, no delivery to schedule, and nothing about the output that depends on the sky. For a load that has to answer requests continuously, that single property is worth more than a lower headline cost per unit.

Where every other renewable breaks against a data center

A data center is the least forgiving load on the grid: continuous, weather-indifferent, and expected to hold better than 99.99% uptime. Measured against that profile, the renewables gap is not close.

A binary plant reinjects the geothermal fluid in a closed loop, so direct emissions at the plant are near zero and the lifecycle figure is dominated by construction rather than operation. At 50 MW running at a 95% capacity factor — roughly 416 GWh a year — choosing geothermal over natural gas avoids on the order of 170,000 to 180,000 tonnes of CO₂ every year, and the advantage compounds for every year the asset keeps running.

Dual use

The same resource that powers the racks also cools them

Cooling is 30 to 40% of what a data center consumes. It is the one overhead that a power contract normally cannot touch — unless the power happens to come from the ground.

Ground-source heat pumps

Ground temperature at 10 to 15 metres holds near 10 to 15°C all year. Heat pumps working against it reach coefficients of performance of 4 to 6, against 2.5 to 3.5 for air-source, and cut cooling energy by 40 to 60%. It works anywhere, not only on a volcanic province.

Absorption cooling

Waste heat from generation drives absorption chillers directly, displacing electrically driven mechanical cooling. In the 4.5 to 13.5 MW range that saves several million kWh a year, and it pays back hardest in exactly the hot climates where conventional cooling is worst.

What it does to PUE

Legacy mechanical cooling runs 1.4 to 1.8. Ground-source brings it to 1.1–1.2, and full geothermal integration in a cold climate approaches 1.03. Every tenth of a point is money and carbon removed from every hour the building operates.

Powering and cooling from one resource is the part that does not show up in a price-per-megawatt-hour comparison. Captured properly it improves project economics by 20 to 40%.

Fuel cost

Zero, and it stays zero for thirty to fifty years

No fuel to price. The heat source is the Earth. There is no commodity exposure to hedge and no fuel escalator to negotiate into a long-term contract.

Operating cost is predictable. Annual operations and maintenance typically runs 2 to 5% of installed cost, and it does not move with a fuel market or a utility rate case.

The asset is long. Thirty to fifty years and beyond, which turns a build decision into multi-decade price certainty rather than a bet on the next rate cycle.

The machine in the photograph

Binary cycle, closed loop, built at data center scale

The plant at the top of this page is a binary cycle unit, and it is the technology that matters most here. It is also the reason the resource does not have to be exotic to be useful.

Moderate temperature. It works on resources of 100 to 180°C, which is an ordinary temperature across a great deal of the western United States.

Closed loop. A secondary organic working fluid runs the turbine and the geothermal fluid is fully reinjected, so nothing is vented and direct emissions are near zero.

The right size already. Typical units run 1 to 50 MW. That is not a plant a data center has to be scaled up to meet; it is a plant a data center already fits.

Direction of travel

The resource is getting cheaper while the demand is getting larger

Capacity is climbing

Global geothermal capacity is expected to reach 25 to 30 GW by 2030, from roughly 16 GW in 2023, and the Department of Energy's GeoVision target is 60 GW in the United States by 2050.

Drilling is the cost, and drilling is changing

Wells dominate the capital. Non-mechanical drilling methods now in development could reduce well cost by 50 to 70%, which moves the economics of every project that has not been drilled yet.

Deeper resources are opening

Reaching supercritical rock above 400°C could yield five to ten times the power per well. The resource has not changed; what reaches it is changing quickly.

Meanwhile the major hyperscalers have committed to round-the-clock carbon-free power on timelines that arrive before the end of this decade. Firm clean generation is the scarce thing in that equation, and there is not much of it to go around.

Why we develop it

Baseload for an industry that has run out of it

The reason we work on geothermal at all is the shortage on the load side. The data center industry is resource bound: the sites it wants are constrained by power long before they are constrained by land, capital or demand, and what it is short of specifically is firm baseload — generation that is there at four in the morning in February without a battery or a favorable forecast behind it. Geothermal is one of the few resources that simply is.

We are not trying to build a large data center. What we develop alongside the resource is remote, localized capacity sited at the generating facility itself — individual projects in the range of 2 to 30 MW, taking power where it is made instead of moving it to where a campus would rather be.

The other half of the table

The same facility, fitted inside a building somebody else already built

Everything above describes a new building on a pad. A large share of what we actually take is not land — it is three thousand square feet inside an existing industrial shell that is already powered. Every figure in the table survives the move except the ones that belong to the shell.

What travels is the inside. The electrical room, the distribution, the cooling plant, the racks and the fire and acoustic treatment are the standard package, and they are the same whether the walls around them are ours or forty years old. That is what makes the engineering paid-for-once claim hold on a retrofit and not just on a pad.

What the shell has to bring is short and unforgiving. Fourteen feet clear to the underside of structure, a floor that carries 250 lb per square foot, three-phase service already energized at the size we need, a wall or roof path for the cooling loop, and yard room outside for the dry coolers. A building that misses any one of those is not a cheaper site, it is a different project.

The trade is schedule against control. A retrofit skips the pad, the shell, the grading and most of the permitting, which is the fastest route to a first operating facility we have. What it costs is the freedom to draw the yard, the fiber entry and the equipment path exactly as the standard says — those get designed around what is already there, one site at a time.

So the specifications above are read two ways. On a greenfield or an alternative-powered pad they are the building. Inside an existing facility they are the suite, and the shell rows — building area, sound at the property line, the yard — are the ones that get verified on site rather than drawn. How those buildings are found and screened is on Site Search.

Site selection is a data problem before it is a real estate problem. We do the narrowing in software, against public records that nobody has bothered to join together, and we spend field time only on what survives.

A conventional search starts with a broker and a handful of listings, which means it starts with the small set of buildings somebody happened to be marketing. We start from the opposite end: the whole population of candidate parcels and buildings in a region, scored against the metrics that actually decide whether a small data center can be built there, and reduced by ranking rather than by chance.

One — build the universe

Federal, state and county records pulled into a single keyed map: the EPA facility registry and its enforcement records, the Department of Energy's industrial assessment database with measured plant energy use, state closure and layoff notices, public transmission and substation geography, county assessor and parcel files, and commercial listings that publish amperage and clear height. Every record is keyed to a parcel, so sources that were never meant to speak to each other can be cross-referenced against the same piece of ground.

Two — score against the metrics

Each candidate is scored on the handful of measures that decide the outcome: electric service already energized and at what size, distance and voltage of the nearest substation, lit fiber, clear height, floor loading, floodplain position, zoning, environmental record, and room for an equipment yard. Weighted, not averaged — a site is not compensated for missing power by having good zoning.

Three — rank, then cut

The scoring produces a ranked database rather than a verdict, which is the point: the threshold can be moved and the whole population re-sorts. Anything failing a fatal screen is dropped outright. What remains is a finalist list short enough to visit.

Four — field analysis

Only finalists earn a site visit. Structural inspection, a read of the actual service and switchgear, sound and setback measured on the ground, and the conversations that no dataset holds — with the utility, the local authority and the owner. Field findings go back into the database, so what we learn on one site re-ranks every other.

Five — validate upward

The surviving sites are proven in writing, in ascending order of cost: a written fiber quote, a utility capacity study and will-serve letter naming the megawatts, a Phase I environmental assessment, a structural report. Every claim that started as a data point ends as a document, or the site drops out.

Existing buildings

The best site is usually one somebody else already powered

A large share of what we look for is not land. It is a building that already has more electric service than its current use needs — a plant that closed, a facility that shrank, an operation that was sized for a load it no longer runs. The slow and expensive work was finished years ago by somebody else, and what is left is fitting it to a much smaller, much denser use than it was built for.

This is where the schedule is actually won. New utility transformers and switchgear are quoted in quarters, not weeks, and a service that is already energized removes that entire queue from the critical path. Buildings in the two to five megawatt band have become genuinely scarce for exactly this reason, and almost nobody is hunting them at our size — the attention is all on sites large enough for hyperscale.

Heavy industrial and process buildings. Printing plants, food and cold storage, fabrication. Reinforced slabs, real floor loading and large clean spans, and service sized for machinery that has already been switched off.

Buildings sized for a load that left. Controlled-environment agriculture, older telecom and process facilities, plants that downsized rather than closed. Service in the low megawatts, well matched to a building that starts at 500 kW and grows.

Sites beside generation. Industrial parcels adjacent to plants that are already generating and already interconnected, where power can be taken on site rather than moved across transmission.

The discipline this demands is skepticism. Most buildings that look powered are not: the service was reduced after the tenant left, the capacity was never contractually theirs, the switchgear cannot be reused, or the floor will not carry the equipment. That is why the screens below are applied to a retrofit candidate exactly as they are to bare land.

Fatal screens

The gate a candidate passes before it costs anyone a day

These are pass or fail, applied in order, and a site that misses one is not negotiated — it is dropped. Most of them can be evaluated from the database before anyone is contacted, which is what makes screening thousands of sites affordable in the first place.

Service already energized

Three-phase service live at the property today, confirmed by the utility account record — not by a panel schedule. A site that needs a new utility transformer is disqualified, because that lead time now runs longer than the entire rest of the schedule.

Fails on a service that has to be built

Fiber lit, and a second way out

One carrier lit nearby and confirmed by a written quote, plus at least one buildable second path: a ring the carrier already runs, line of sight to a point of presence, or fiber on the host’s transmission corridor. A coverage map is a marketing artifact and is not evidence that anything is in the ground.

Fails on no lit service, or no second path of any kind

Permitted by right

The use is allowed by right under the existing zoning. We do not buy a rezoning fight, and we do not ask a community to change its plan to accommodate us.

Fails on a use that needs a rezoning

Outside the floodplain

Clear of the mapped floodplain, including the 500-year outline, rather than engineered into compliance with it.

Fails on mapped flood risk

Clear height and a clean span

Enough clear height for the equipment, with no columns landing where the rows need to run. A structure that has to be cut apart is a structure we did not need to buy.

Fails on columns through the hall

Room for the equipment yard

Side or rear yard for generation and cooling, positioned and screened so the sound stays inside the property line at the neighborhood limit.

Fails on no yard, or no room to screen it

Clean environmental history

A clean Phase I environmental assessment. Findings are not a price negotiation; they are a reason to walk.

Fails on a recognized environmental condition

Why it matters here

Development is mostly reading, joining and rebuilding — and that is the part a machine is good at

Most of what a development company actually does all day is not judgment. It is reading public records that were never meant to be read together, joining them to a piece of ground, chasing a document that proves what a data point claimed, rebuilding a spreadsheet because one assumption moved, and writing the same paragraph into the ninth version of a report. Conventionally that work is staffed — analysts, a GIS contractor, a cost consultant, a bookkeeper, a designer, a document manager — and the staffing is why small projects are said not to pencil.

Site selection

Thousands of candidates ranked before anyone drives anywhere

Site search is the clearest case. It is a data problem before it is a real estate problem, and we run it as one: a database and a re-runnable harness rather than a broker and a shortlist. The full method is set out on the Site Search page; what belongs here is the machinery underneath it.

The universe is built from closures

A candidate list is assembled from state closure and layoff notices, the federal facility registry and its enforcement history, the Department of Energy's industrial assessment records with measured plant energy use, county assessor and parcel files, public transmission and substation geography, and listings that publish amperage and clear height. The notices arrive as scanned PDFs; they are parsed to structured records automatically, because a building's utility service decays on a clock and the ones worth finding are the ones found early.

Everything keys to a parcel

Each record from each source is resolved to the same piece of ground, so sources that have never spoken to one another can be cross-examined against one parcel. The join is the whole value; without it, six datasets are six lists.

Screens are pass or fail, and they run first

Service already energized, substation distance and voltage, floodplain position, zoning, structure, yard, environmental record. Applied in order, evaluated from the database before anyone is contacted, and a candidate that misses one is dropped rather than negotiated. Screening thousands of sites is only affordable because this part costs nothing but compute.

Weighted, then ranked, then cut

Survivors are scored on the measures that decide the outcome, weighted rather than averaged — a site is not compensated for missing power by having good zoning. The output is a ranked database, not a verdict, so a threshold can be moved and the whole population re-sorts in seconds.

A new search area is a configuration file

Opening a region means writing down a center, a radius, the jurisdictions and their public data endpoints, and one translator per county. It is not a new project and it is not a new hire. Reno is live; Klamath Falls and Ukiah are queued behind it.

Every run is repeatable

Each command is idempotent, each run is logged, and every network call is cached, so a search can be re-run months later and the difference between the two runs is the answer — which sites moved, which service was reduced, which building came to market.

Mapping and resources

The ground, the grid and the heat, on one map

A site decision is a geometry problem: where the parcel sits relative to the floodplain, the substation, the transmission corridor, the fault, the neighbor and the resource. We compute those relationships rather than eyeball them off a viewer.

Parcel and hazard geometry. Parcel boundaries against floodplain polygons, setbacks, access and yard area, computed exactly. Where a precise geometry library is unavailable the tools fall back to bounding boxes and say so — a fallback that is conservative in the safe direction, able to turn a pass into a fail but never a fail into a pass.

Grid geography. Substation location, capacity and voltage, transmission and distribution routing, distance from the parcel to the point of service, and regional price and load data, joined to the same parcel key as everything else.

Geothermal resource. Measured well temperatures, heat-flow and gradient data, producing fields and their published performance, land status, and the state and federal survey record for a basin — assembled into a resource picture for a search area before a single lease is discussed.

Maps are generated, not drawn. Every figure comes out of the dataset that produced the conclusion, so a map cannot drift away from the numbers it illustrates. When the data updates, the map is regenerated rather than redrawn.

Documents and due diligence

Fifty years of paper, made searchable

The most valuable geothermal information in the West is old, public, and effectively unreadable: state survey bulletins, federal resource assessments, well logs, decades-old feasibility studies, utility filings. It is scanned, it is not indexed, and it is why the same basin gets re-studied every fifteen years by somebody who could not find what was already known about it.

We run that corpus through optical character recognition and into a full-text index, so a question about a field, a well, a temperature or a flow rate returns the page it is answered on. Facts extracted from those documents stay attached to the document, the revision and the date they came from. Where two sources disagree — and on a fifty-year record they frequently do — the conflict is surfaced as a conflict rather than quietly averaged into a number that no source supports.

Analysis

The numbers are generated, not typed

Behind the reports sits an analysis server: a single queryable body of costs, rates, comparables and assumptions that every document and every model reads from. Capital cost by component, operating cost, construction rates, drilling and plant figures, market benchmarks, grid prices, transaction comparables, the tax credit and grant landscape, after-tax treatment, capital structure and valuation, and a risk register carried alongside them.

One consequence matters more than the rest: an assumption is stated in exactly one place. Moving it re-runs everything downstream instead of being pasted into thirty cells across six files, three of which will be missed. The failure mode of conventional development analysis is not bad math — it is nine versions of a spreadsheet that no longer agree, and nobody knowing which one is current.

Pro formas

A pro forma that can be re-run, compared and defended

The financial model is a program rather than a workbook. Any figure in it is addressable, every figure carries the source and the assumption behind it, and a variant is a named set of changes rather than a copied file.

Variants, side by side. Capacity, phasing, resource assumption, financing shape and site type are parameters. Two cases are compared by the tool, on the same basis and the same assumptions, with the differences listed rather than hunted.

Sensitivity is cheap. Because a case costs seconds, the question stops being "what does the model say" and becomes "what has to be true for this to work" — which is the only version of the question worth asking a lender or a partner.

Capital cost from components. Equipment, drilling, plant, construction, interconnection and building are priced from current rates and equipment bid sheets, not from a dollars-per-megawatt rule of thumb inherited from a project at ten times the size.

Assumptions are a document. Every case can print the assumption register that produced it. A reviewer does not have to reverse-engineer the model to find out what it believes.

Accounting

Books that keep up with a development schedule

Development spending is awkward to account for: it is lumpy, it splits between what is capitalized into a project and what is expensed, it lands across entities and phases, and it arrives as vendor invoices and bids rather than tidy categories. We run the assembly of that work with the same tooling — spend classified to project and phase, vendor and bid records kept beside the cost model, and cost-to-date reconciled against the capital estimate so the estimate and the ledger are the same conversation instead of two documents that meet once a year.

The point is the loop, not the bookkeeping. When what a thing actually cost flows back into the model that estimated it, the next estimate is better; when it does not, every project is priced by memory.

Reports and deliverables

Written once, regenerated thereafter

Every study, memorandum and deck this company issues is produced by a generator rather than by hand. The consequences are unglamorous and worth a great deal.

One house style, enforced

Cover, header, footer, confidentiality line, the instruction to verify before relying, the source revision and date, and page numbering are applied by the generator to every document. There is no version with the old logo, because there is no hand-made version.

Reissue is a re-run

When an underlying figure changes, the affected documents are regenerated in minutes, in Word and PDF, from the same corrected source. The alternative — finding every document that quoted the old number — is the reason stale figures survive in practice.

A library with a manifest

The finished studies sit in one indexed library, and a manifest records for every file its source, its revision, its date and an automated check that it carries the right marks. It is checkable rather than asserted.

The reader gets the provenance

Each document says on its face which revision of which source it was built from and on what date. A reader never has to ask whether they are holding the current one.

Design and engineering

The building is modeled before it is drawn

The facility is a standard kit repeated, which makes a three-dimensional model an asset that improves rather than a drawing thrown away at the end of a job. We build and revise those models directly from the specification — the equipment layout, the yard, the plant, the shell and its regional variants, setbacks and sound — and every project the kit is used on sharpens the model for the next one.

This is where a repeated format pays off twice. Design work that a one-off building spends and discards is spent once here and amortized across every site, which is a large part of why a 500 kW building can be developed economically at all.

Project management

The schedule is a system, not a status meeting

The work itself is run through a task harness. Every piece of work is a record with an owner, a stated done-condition and a claim on the files it touches, so two workstreams never quietly edit the same thing and discover it later. A task closes against its done-condition with evidence, not against somebody's opinion that it is finished. Long-lead items — utility studies, interconnection, permits, equipment orders — are tracked against dates, because on a project this size the schedule is decided almost entirely by the three or four items nobody can accelerate.

Automated checks refuse work that does not meet the standard: a claim without a source, a document missing its legend, a change that would break the published site. The standard is enforced by the tooling rather than remembered by a person under time pressure, which is the only place a standard survives.

Software and this website

The tools are ours, including this page

This site is generated and validated by our own build, with the rules of the page — contrast, the accent colors, the required legal legend, what a page may and may not contain — checked by software that refuses to publish a version that breaks them. The same is true of the site database, the analysis server and the document generators. Changing the method means editing a file, not filing a request with a vendor and waiting a quarter.

The discipline

Every claim declares what it rests on

Machine-assisted work is worth having only if it is more reliable than the work it replaces, and a language model left unsupervised is confidently wrong in exactly the register that reads as authoritative. So the tooling carries rules, and the rules are the reason the output is usable.

Primary or inferred, stated

A finding rests on the artifact itself — a file, a query result, a page of a document, a measured value — or it rests on reasoning from packaging: a title, a summary, an assumption, another tool's output. The second kind is a hypothesis and is labeled one. It is never acted on until it is promoted by primary evidence.

A tool that errored produces no facts

When a query returns nothing, that is a fact about the query until a second method agrees. "The record does not exist" and "the search failed" look identical in the output and are opposite conclusions; treating the first as established without checking is how confident nonsense enters a report.

Conflicts are surfaced, not smoothed

Where two sources disagree, both are kept and the disagreement is named. An averaged number that no source supports is worse than either of the numbers that produced it.

Verification does not delegate

Load-bearing quotes and figures are checked against the source by a person — particularly any finding that reverses an earlier conclusion. The machine assembles the case; it does not get to certify it.

Data-room ready by construction

Because every claim is already tied to its document, the diligence package is not a three-week assembly exercise at the end. It is the same chain of sources the work was done on, printed.

Limits

What the machines do not do

The boundary is not modesty; it is where the value actually sits. None of this negotiates a purchase or a lease, talks to a utility engineer, walks a roof, reads a switchgear nameplate, obtains a will-serve letter, sits with a planning commission, signs anything, or decides how capital is committed. A ranked database does not produce a fiber quote, a capacity study, a Phase I or a structural report — and a site is not real until it has all four.

What the tooling does is make the expensive human work land on the right small number of things. Field time is spent on finalists rather than on the set of buildings a broker happened to be marketing. Analysis time is spent on the assumption that matters rather than on rebuilding a workbook. A document is read for whether it is right, not retyped.

The machine narrows the field and carries the paperwork. People decide, and people sign.

Rhyolite Geo Inc. builds at the plant itself, on the generator's own property. Why now

The companies that built the largest campuses have published the case for spreading out

A small data center is not a scaled-down version of a large one. It is a shape that became buildable, and nearly all of the engineering that made it buildable was published by the operators running the biggest facilities on earth.

Little of what follows is our argument. It is in the public engineering record — conference papers, architecture guides and the operators' own incident reports — and it has accumulated to the point where the distributed case no longer has to be made from first principles.

Failure isolation became arithmetic. Amazon's published account of shuffle sharding works the combinations out in the open: across eight workers there are twenty-eight unique pairs, so a problem reaches one twenty-eighth of customers rather than a quarter of them. Their cell architecture guidance puts it plainly — a workload split across ten cells keeps ninety per cent of requests unaffected when one cell fails. Spreading load is not a hope about redundancy; it is a number you can compute before you build.

Distance stopped being an obstacle and became a budget. Coherent optics now ship inside an ordinary pluggable transceiver: the industry interoperability agreements for 400G and 800G describe single-span links of eighty to a hundred and twenty kilometres aimed specifically at connecting data centers. Light crosses a kilometre of fiber in about five microseconds, and under roughly fifty kilometres the equipment at each end adds more delay than the glass between them. Sites tens of kilometres apart are, to the traffic, one facility.

What fails together is rarely the building. Google's own report on a 2019 outage records automation that descheduled multiple independent clusters at once, in their words “crucially even if those clusters were in different physical locations.” Redundancy that shares a control plane is not redundancy. That reframes the question from how large a site is to how many genuinely separate things have to go wrong before service stops.

Inference is the workload that wants to be near the person asking. Its constraint is the delay on a single request, not bandwidth between processors. The public industry benchmarks for interactive serving set budgets in the hundreds of milliseconds to first response and tens of milliseconds per word after it. A site in the region meets that budget. A distant campus spends a meaningful share of it on the wire before any work is done.

Spreading storage got cheaper than duplicating it. Meta's published storage work reports cutting effective replication from 3.6 copies to as low as 2.1 while remaining resilient to the loss of a whole data center. Microsoft Research's coding work describes surviving failures at roughly a third more raw capacity, against three times for plain replication. Geographic spread stopped being the expensive option.

Weather correlates generation across whole regions. The federal regulators' final report on the February 2021 freeze found that eighty-one per cent of freeze-related generating outages happened at temperatures above the units' own stated design limits, and that the large majority of fuel-related outages were natural gas. One weather system took thousands of megawatts off one grid. Diversity of geology and of interconnection is a different kind of hedge than diversity of buildings, and it is the one we are positioned to offer.

The honest limit is worth stating, because the counter-argument is real. Training a frontier model is tightly coupled work that wants one building: a single rack-scale interconnect moves data between processors at a rate nothing spanning sites reproduces. We do not claim that workload. What has changed is that even there the ground moved — Microsoft, having built a campus explicitly for it, then published that demand is outpacing the power and space of a single facility and that it now connects sites across geographically diverse locations. The argument for one enormous building is narrower than it was, and it was narrowed by the people making it.

Small sites carry their own costs, and we would rather name them than be caught by them. Supporting infrastructure runs in the millions of dollars per megawatt whether the megawatt is alone or in company, staffing does not fall to zero because a building is unmanned, and the industry's own survey work still finds power the most common cause of serious outages — which is precisely the exposure a small site is least equipped to absorb. Distribution reduces correlated risk. It does not reduce the risk at any one site, and pretending otherwise would be the same overreach the large-campus case is making.

So the tenants this shape suits are specific: regional enterprises, managed service providers, and inference that has to answer near the people asking — capacity in their own region rather than space in a distant campus, and resilience that comes from being spread out rather than from being big in one place.
